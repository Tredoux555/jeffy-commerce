1  The Opportunity

Imported goods are overpriced, online delivery is expensive, and millions need work. Jeffy solves all three at once.

South Africans pay too much for imported goods because of the layers between the factory and the buyer, and online retail stays expensive because last-mile logistics is hard and costly. Meanwhile unemployment sits at roughly 32% — and above 60% for young people — leaving a vast pool of capable people who could earn by selling and delivering in their own communities. Jeffy imports directly and hands delivery to local resellers: the customer gets factory-direct prices, and the reseller gets a real micro-business at a fraction of the capital a shop or courier needs.

Market context: South African online retail is growing roughly 8–9% a year and is projected to reach around R225 billion within five years — a large, expanding market into which a structurally lower-cost model can take share.

2  The Insight — Why the Obvious Model Loses Money

Plain "import and courier to the customer" loses money in South Africa. Jeffy is engineered to fix exactly that.

Most import-to-consumer plays struggle here because two costs quietly eat the margin: paid customer acquisition (advertising to win each order) and last-mile courier delivery. On a typical R249 order, those two alone can turn a healthy gross margin into a per-order loss. This is why even large players lean on heavy subsidy.

The same R249 order goes from a −R18 loss to a +R60 contribution for Jeffy — while the reseller still earns ~R59. That single structural move is the investment thesis.

3  How the Model Works

Jeffy imports and owns the stock; independent resellers buy it wholesale and resell at retail. Both sides earn a healthy margin.

Value chain: Chinese factories → Jeffy (imports, owns landed stock) → independent resellers (buy wholesale, resell at retail) → customers (order on jeffy.co.za). Jeffy collects payment centrally; PayFast Split Payments divides each sale in real time into Jeffy's wholesale revenue and the reseller's margin, so Jeffy never holds a reseller's money.

PrincipleWhat it means
Two-tier marginJeffy earns a mark-up over true landed cost; the reseller earns a mark-up over wholesale. Both are set off real landed cost, so neither side runs at a loss.
Graduation pathNew resellers start on credit (Jeffy-owned stock, retention of title) against a limit; as they build a cash cushion they buy upfront for a bigger margin — steadily reducing Jeffy's credit exposure.
Asset-light for JeffyNo stores, no fleet, no last-mile payroll — those costs and risks sit with resellers, who are paid by their margin.
Buy-sell, not employmentResellers buy and resell at a margin — not employees, not agents (the proven Avon / Tupperware structure). No PAYE, minimum wage or leave on the reseller leg.
Why this will look familiar — and credible — to a Chinese reviewer

Jeffy is the South African expression of three models already proven at massive scale in China:

社区团购 Community group-buying local leaders aggregate neighbourhood demand and handle the last leg — exactly Jeffy's reseller.

代理 / 分销 Distributor model independent agents buy wholesale and resell — Jeffy's two-tier margin.

私域流量 Private-domain traffic demand built through trusted local relationships, not paid ads — Jeffy's Wish List and reseller word-of-mouth.

Pinduoduo, Meituan and the community group-buying wave built tens of billions in value on these mechanics. Jeffy applies them to a market — South Africa — where high unemployment makes the reseller layer abundant and where factory-direct pricing has an unusually large gap to exploit.

4  Why It's Hard to Beat

A dense local network is slow for a pure-online rival to copy — and the structure keeps Jeffy lean and legally clean.

Once resellers are dense in an area, a pure-online competitor cannot easily match them on delivery cost or local trust. Selling factory-direct also undercuts the importers and retailers stacked above the factory price, while VAT registration lets Jeffy recover the 15% import VAT that an unregistered importer eats as a permanent cost. The result is a price the incumbents structurally cannot reach without rebuilding their whole chain.

Honest note on the moat & structure. The legal scaffolding — buy-sell (not employment), disclosed-agent collection, no joining fee (keeps it outside Consumer Protection Act franchise rules), and VAT registration — is sound in principle and modelled on established businesses, but must be finalised with the company's attorney and chartered accountant before resellers are signed. These are confirmable items, not open questions, and the cost of doing so is in the Tranche-1 budget.

Two conditions we are explicit about
  • VAT registration is a committed action, not an assumption. Jeffy is not yet VAT-registered; unregistered, the 15% import VAT (~R12/unit) is a permanent cost and landed cost is ~R92. Is ~R59 enough to keep a reseller?

At maturity the model assumes ~120–150 active resellers serving 18,000 orders/month — about 90–150 orders each per month, or roughly R5,300–R8,800 in monthly margin for part-time, local, low-capital work with no fixed costs. That is attractive supplementary income in a high-unemployment market, and the graduation path (buy upfront for a bigger margin) rewards the best performers. 6  The Wish List — Demand & Marketing in One

Customers tell Jeffy what to stock before a cent is spent — and the weekly giveaway markets the brand for almost nothing.

Before buying any stock, Jeffy asks customers what they want. Anyone can submit one to ten products they wish they had — no purchase, no catch. Each week Jeffy draws winners at random and grants the wish (sourced and delivered free), announcing every winner on radio, in local press, and across social media.

It de-risks inventory

The wishes are a live map of real demand. Jeffy stocks what people have already asked for, so it buys into proven want — minimising dead stock, the single biggest way an import business loses cash.

It markets for almost nothing

Each "wish granted" is a public, human, shareable moment — turning marketing into a weekly community event at a fraction of paid-ad cost, and feeding the private-domain, word-of-mouth demand the model relies on.

This is also why the first hire is a Social / Community Manager. The Wish List, the weekly winners, the socials and reseller communications are a real job — and the role that most directly drives revenue. It is funded from day one out of central overhead (see page 8).

Brand-safety note

The programme is branded "Jeffy Wish List" and is a no-purchase weekly random draw (not a vote). It deliberately never uses the phrase "Make-A-Wish," which is a protected trademark. Promotional-competition rules under the Consumer Protection Act will be followed (published rules, no purchase required).

  • Equity, not a loan. R5.0m buys a 50% shareholding in Jeffy Commerce (Pty) Ltd, registered at CIPC, with funds introduced from abroad through proper exchange-control (SARB) channels.

Why this structure protects the investor's downside. Capital is staged against proof, two-thirds is recoverable inventory rather than sunk spend, and the equity is genuine and retained — aligning the founder and investor on the same outcome: a profitable, growing company.

Build the foundations once, properly — then replicate fast. This is an empire, not a get-rich-quick scheme.

Each rung is only climbed once the rung below is proven. That discipline is what makes the staged capital safe: Jeffy spends small to prove the model works in one place, then pours capital only into what has already been shown to sell profitably. The mechanics are deliberately repeatable — once one area works, a second and third are copies of a known recipe, which is why a careful build in the early years can scale quickly later.

11  Stock, Storage, Insurance & Liability

Jeffy owns the stock, so Jeffy insures it — and the model is designed to keep that risk small, capped and covered.

Because resellers hold Jeffy-owned stock at home and deliver from there, three things are handled deliberately rather than left to chance: where stock is stored, how it is insured, and who carries a loss.

1 · Storage & zoning
  • The bulk of inventory sits in a central / regional micro-warehouse; resellers hold only small, fast-moving rolling stock — keeping each home within municipal "home-occupation" limits and cutting concentration risk.
  • Per-reseller stock is capped by the credit limit, so no single residential address holds large value.
  • Resellers confirm they may store & deliver from their property (owner / landlord / body-corporate rules). Food lines are avoided unless a municipal licence is held.
2 · Insurance (carried by Jeffy)
  • Jeffy holds title, so Jeffy carries a central commercial "stock at various premises" policy plus goods-in-transit cover — not the reseller's home policy, which excludes business stock.
  • The per-reseller value cap keeps the cover insurable and bounds any single claim.
  • Product-liability cover for Jeffy's importer exposure under the Consumer Protection Act (s.61 strict liability).
  • Premiums sit in monthly overhead — affordable even in the stress case (page 7).
3 · Who carries a loss — and the honest limit of insurance

Stock moves under retention of title with a per-reseller ledger and inventory records; theft claims require a SAPS case number. Insurance covers fire, burglary and transit loss. The hardest exposure — a reseller selling stock and claiming theft, or disappearing with it — is only partly insurable, because dishonesty by the person entrusted is excluded from ordinary cover. It is therefore managed operationally: vetting, small starting limits, graduation to larger limits, and a modest bad-debt allowance built into the model rather than assumed away.

To be finalised before launch (Tranche-1 budget): exact policy wordings with a commercial broker (stock-throughput + goods-in-transit + product liability), the reseller agreement with an attorney (title, security, loss allocation), and a zoning check with a town planner. These are confirmable, already-budgeted items — not legal or broking advice.
