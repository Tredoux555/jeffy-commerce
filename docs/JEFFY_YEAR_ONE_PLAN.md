# Jeffy — How It Works in Year One

*A plain description of the plan for the first twelve months.*

## The short version

Year one is about promotion, not scale. We are not trying to build a big catalogue or a wide store. We are trying to prove one thing: that the Wish List system works, that people enter it, that winners are real and filmable, and that the whole loop — from a product landing in stock to a winner holding it — runs without breaking. We do that quietly, in one place, with a small set of products I already know and use.

## The Wish List system

The Wish List is the engine. It is simple. A person browses the products Jeffy stocks and adds the ones they want to their wish list. Those wishes do two jobs at once. First, they tell us what people actually want, which is how we decide what to import next — demand becomes our sourcing signal. Second, every wish is an entry into a draw.

Each month we run one random draw and one person wins the item they wished for. We film the handover, with their consent and with dignity, and that becomes the content we post. One genuine wish granted, done properly, turns into eight to fifteen pieces of shareable content. That is the growth model: we do not buy reach, we earn it by giving real things to real people and letting the story travel.

**Draw cadence: monthly, for now.** A monthly draw keeps operations light while we are still finding and fixing problems, builds anticipation between draws, and lets one promoter and one test store handle it comfortably. We move to a weekly draw only once entry volume is high enough that a month feels too slow. Small prizes suit a faster cadence eventually, but weekly roughly triples the work, so it is a decision for later in the year, not the start.

## Prizes

Prizes stay small and useful in year one. No fridges, no televisions, no big-ticket items. Think gym gloves, a salt and pepper grinder, a magnetic kitchen strip — things that are genuinely nice to win, cheap enough to give away often, and easy to ship and film. Keeping prizes small means the budget stretches, the logistics stay simple, and we can give away more often without strain.

## The flagship test store

We run the whole system through one person, in one location — most likely Johannesburg. This is the flagship store and the test bed. Their job is to live with the system end to end: receive stock, list products, process entries, run a draw, hand over a prize, and tell us everything that went wrong along the way. Every bug, every confusing step, every bit of friction gets found here before we ever try to repeat the model elsewhere. We do not expand until this one store runs clean.

## The starting products

The first shipment is built from products I personally use and would happily stand behind. Starting with things I know means I can speak to them honestly and spot quality problems myself. The opening range:

- Camping chair
- Salt and pepper grinder
- M128 bow
- Mobile battery-powered compressor
- Magnetic kitchen strip
- Knives
- Archery releases
- Spotlights
- Gym gloves

**Budget for this first shipment: R50,000.** That covers the opening stock across these lines — enough to fill the test store, supply early prizes, and have product on hand to film. We treat this as the year-one inventory experiment, not a commitment to reorder everything; what sells and what people wish for tells us what the second shipment should be.

## The promotions manager

The Wish List needs someone running it day to day, and that person is the promotions manager. The role is to manage entries, run the monthly draw, coordinate the prize handover, and produce the content that comes out of it — filming, posting, and keeping the social channels alive between draws.

A university student is the right fit: hungry, social-media native, flexible, and affordable. We budget **R100,000 per year, paid monthly** (roughly R8,300 a month). This is an independent contractor arrangement, not employment — they invoice us each month and manage their own hours and obligations.

*One note on doing it cleanly:* even as a contractor under a six-figure annual figure, it is worth keeping this tidy from day one — a simple written contractor agreement, a monthly invoice, and basic records. South African contractors still carry their own tax responsibilities, and SARS looks at the substance of a working relationship, not just the label. Keeping clean records costs nothing now and saves trouble later. It is not about staying under anyone's radar; it is about being able to show the arrangement is exactly what it says it is.

## How the year runs

**Months 1–3 — Stand it up.** First shipment lands. Flagship store is set up and stocked. Promotions manager is hired and trained on the system. We run the first monthly draws, film the first handovers, and start posting. The goal here is not growth — it is finding everything that breaks.

**Months 4–8 — Tighten and repeat.** With the obvious bugs out, the focus shifts to making each monthly cycle smoother and the content sharper. We learn which products people actually wish for and start planning the second shipment around real demand rather than my guesses. Social following starts to compound off the back of consistent, dignified winner stories.

**Months 9–12 — Decide what scales.** By now we know whether the loop works, what it costs to run, and what people want. This is when we decide whether to keep the cadence monthly or move to weekly, whether to widen the product range, and whether a second store is worth standing up. Year one ends with a proven system and the evidence to grow it.

## What success looks like

Year one is a success if, by month twelve, the system runs without us babysitting it: stock comes in, wishes come in, a winner is drawn and filmed every month, the content goes out, and the following is growing. We are not measuring it in revenue. We are measuring it in whether the machine works and whether the story sells. If it does, year two is about turning it up.
